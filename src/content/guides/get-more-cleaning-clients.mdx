Almost every article on this subject gives you the same list. Social media, flyers, word of mouth,
a website. All true, all useless, because none of it tells you what to do on Tuesday.

Here is the same territory ordered by what actually returns clients for a small residential
cleaning business, with the specific version of each rather than the category.

## 1. Google Business Profile, properly filled in

This is the highest return thing most cleaning businesses are half doing.

When someone searches "house cleaning near me", the map results appear above everything else. That
placement is free, and it is decided largely by things you control.

What actually moves it:

- **Complete every field.** Service area, hours, services list, description. Half completed
  profiles rank below complete ones.
- **Photos, regularly.** Not stock images. Real before and after shots from real jobs. Add some
  every month rather than twenty once.
- **Reviews, asked for consistently.** This is the biggest single factor and the one most owners
  neglect. Ask every satisfied client, every time, at the moment they say something nice.
- **Reply to every review**, including the bad ones, calmly and briefly. Prospects read the
  replies more carefully than the reviews.
- **Put a booking link on the profile.** A prospect who has decided should be able to act
  immediately rather than being told to call during business hours.

That last point matters more than it sounds. Most cleaning enquiries happen in the evening, after
the person has finished a long day and decided they are done cleaning their own house. If the
only option is calling you tomorrow, a proportion of them never do.

## 2. Ask your existing clients, out loud

Referrals are the highest quality clients you will ever get. They arrive pre-trusted, they haggle
less, and they stay longer.

Almost nobody asks for them systematically.

The version that works is specific and low pressure, delivered when a client has just expressed
satisfaction:

> "I'm glad you're happy with it. I've got room for one or two more houses in this area. If you
> know anyone who's been meaning to get help, I'd really appreciate the introduction."

Note what that does. It is specific about capacity, specific about geography, and it gives them a
reason to think of a particular person rather than "anyone".

A referral incentive helps but is not the main thing. A free clean or a credit for both parties is
common. The main thing is asking at all.

## 3. Get clustered, not spread out

This is a growth tactic disguised as a routing tactic.

Five clients in one neighbourhood are worth far more than five clients across a city, because your
unpaid drive time collapses. The same working day fits more paid hours.

So when you ask for referrals, ask the clients whose neighbourhoods you want more of. When you
advertise, advertise where you already work. When you price, consider that a house two doors from
an existing client is genuinely cheaper for you to serve.

Density is the difference between a full day of five cleans and an exhausting day of three.

## 4. Local Facebook groups, without being a nuisance

Every town has neighbourhood groups where "can anyone recommend a cleaner?" gets posted weekly.

The people who win that work are not the ones posting adverts. They are the ones already in the
group, whose name a neighbour mentions in the replies.

Practically: join the groups for the areas you serve, answer the recommendation posts when they
appear, and do not post promotional content unless the group explicitly allows it. Being the
person who replies helpfully within an hour beats any advert.

## 5. Property managers and realtors

One relationship here can be worth a dozen residential clients, because they buy repeatedly and
they are not price shopping, they are reliability shopping.

Realtors need move-out and listing cleans on short notice. Property managers need turnovers. Both
have the same problem: the cleaner they used last time did not answer the phone.

The pitch is not that you are cheap. It is that you answer, you show up, and you send photo proof
the property was ready. That is what they are actually buying.

## 6. Short term rental hosts

Similar economics, different rhythm. A host with three units needs cleans several times a week,
forever, and switching cleaners is painful for them, so they stay.

They care about the same thing property managers care about, plus timing, because a missed
turnover costs them a guest review.

## What to skip

**Flyers and door hangers.** Enormous effort, very low return, and the response you do get skews
heavily toward price shoppers.

**Paid ads before your profile and reviews are solid.** You will pay to send traffic to a business
that looks less established than the competitor next to you. Fix the free thing first.

**Discounting to win volume.** It works, briefly, and it fills your schedule with the clients most
likely to leave. Being busy at a bad rate is worse than having gaps.

## The thing that keeps them

Getting clients is the visible problem. Keeping them is the one that decides whether the business
works.

Most residential cleaning clients leave for reasons that are entirely preventable: inconsistent
quality between cleaners, missed visits, awkwardness about an invoice, or one unresolved complaint
about a room.
